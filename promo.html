finding_numbers

navigate by ear_

A liminal horror maze that plays through your ears. A shortwave number station bleeds through the static — and some of these rooms don't stay where you left them.

Nothing chases you here. Nothing kills you. The station's silence is the whole threat, and losing it is the only way to lose.

▸ What you're listening for is whether a number arrives at all. The game can't be played without sound.

What comes through

A whole number station, built to be listened to

RX · EAR

Navigate by ear

No map, nothing marks the right door. A correct turn adds a number to the broadcast, a wrong one adds nothing — no buzzer, no red flash. Most rooms hold still; some move their exit before settling.

RX · VOICE

A station of its own

Built in WebAudio: spoken digits in six languages over a brown-noise bed that circles the signal without ever swallowing it. A tribute, not a recording.

RX · 1977

Half of this would have run in 1977

The screen is 23×20 characters, which fits inside the 40×24 text mode the Apple II shipped with in June 1977. The rules are 55 lines — two counters and a score formula — and the maze is never stored, only generated a room at a time. That much is period hardware.

The sound is not. That took the next 49 years — and it's the part you play.

RX · CRT

A character-mode monitor

One font, one phosphor color, one glyph grid — a text-mode screen bent through a WebGL CRT filter, the signal scrolling as a live spectrogram. Dial the decay up until the picture barely holds.

RX · DEPTH

16 levels

The message grows from three digits to fourteen, and the station tightens its gaps to match, so a longer message never buys you more time. What's past the sixteenth isn't documented.
